# Nifty 50 over ten years: price, P/E and earnings (September 2016 to September 2026)

Latest reading 28 September 2026. Companion files: `nifty_decade_price_pe_earnings.xlsx` (data, formulas and native Excel charts) and `nifty_decade_price_pe_earnings.png` (the chart).

## The one thing to remember

A P/E ratio tells you how many rupees investors pay for each rupee of a company's yearly profit. NSE publishes one for the Nifty 50 every day: the index level divided by the profit its companies reported over the last four quarters. Dividing the index by that P/E gives the profit (or earnings) behind each unit of the index. Think of the Nifty as one share; this is the profit that one share earned over the past year.

The last ten years split into two halves. Until 2019, the Nifty rose mainly because investors paid more for each rupee of profit, while profits grew slowly. Since then, it has risen because profits grew fast, and investors have ended up paying less for each rupee. Over the whole decade the two roughly cancel out: the Nifty went from 8,611 to 22,780, which is 2.6 times, and the profit behind it grew about 2.7 to 2.8 times.

## Takeaways

**1. NSE changed how it counts profit in 2021, and that breaks most long-run comparisons.**
Until March 2021, NSE worked out the Nifty's P/E from each company's standalone profit, the profit of the parent company alone. From 31 March 2021 it switched to consolidated profit, which also counts the profit of subsidiaries, the other companies the parent owns or controls. Consolidated profit was about 20% higher that day, so the P/E dropped from 40.4x to 33.2x overnight while the index moved about 1%. (A P/E of 40.4x means investors paid ₹40.4 for every ₹1 of yearly profit.) The chart shows the years before the switch in a lighter shade. Every "like-for-like" figure below adds the missing subsidiary profit back to the pre-2021 numbers so the two periods can be compared.

**2. From 2016 to 2019, prices rose mostly because investors paid more; profits grew only slowly.**
Between September 2016 and December 2019, the Nifty rose 41%, from 8,611 to 12,168. The profit behind each unit rose 17%, from ₹368 to ₹430, about 5% a year, and half of that came in one step when the corporate tax rate was cut in September 2019 (on NSE's figures, profit per unit went from about ₹404 at the end of August to ₹435 at the end of September). The rest of the gain came from the P/E, which climbed from 23.4x to 28.3x on NSE's old counting method. PrimeInvestor wrote in December 2019 that Nifty earnings had grown "barely 2 per cent a year" over the previous five years, and ICICI Direct, looking back in its 2024 strategy report, puts Nifty earnings growth at 2% a year over FY17 to FY20.

**3. From 2020 to 2026, profits did the work and the P/E came down.**
Between December 2019 and September 2026, the Nifty rose 87%, from 12,168 to 22,780. Over the same stretch, the profit behind each unit grew 2.3 to 2.4 times, like-for-like. For both to be true, the P/E had to fall, and it did. In December 2019 it was somewhere between 23.5x and 25x on today's counting method; today it is 19.3x, a drop of 18% to 22%. The path was bumpy: prices recovered from the COVID crash before profits did, so the P/E first shot up to its highest level of the decade in early 2021, and then came down as profits caught up. Motilal Oswal's own tally shows the same upswing, with Nifty earnings growing more than 20% a year from FY20 to FY24.

**4. Today's P/E is low against the last five years, and the usual "10-year average" overstates how low.**
Since the 2021 switch, the median month-end P/E is 22.0x; that is the middle reading when every month-end is lined up from lowest to highest. Today's 19.3x (28 September) is 12.5% below it and lower than any month-end reading since the switch; the lowest month-end before now was 19.5x in June 2022, though on individual days that month it probably dipped to about 19x. Some widely quoted 10-year averages, such as Business Standard's 26.7x (January 2025) and Craytheon's 23.28x, include the higher readings from before 2021. Averages built that way make today's P/E look further below normal than it really is.

**5. Like-for-like, investors have paid less for each rupee of profit than today in only two stretches of the decade.**
Once the pre-2021 P/Es are put on today's counting method, readings below today's 19.3x show up in the COVID crash of spring 2020 (about 16x to 17x in March) and, on the main estimates, in the months after demonetisation in late 2016, running into early 2017 (about 18x to 19x). Both came after big shocks to the market. Two episodes are too few to predict anything from, so the safe conclusion is a modest one: on this measure, the market sits near the bottom of its ten-year range.

**6. The trailing P/E and the forward P/E answer different questions.**
NSE's P/E divides today's price by the profit already reported over the past year, which is why it is called the trailing P/E; it stands at 19.3x. The forward P/E divides the same price by the profit analysts expect over the next twelve months. Bloomberg's forward P/E, from the chart in our earlier analysis, stands at about 16.9x, and Motilal Oswal's at 17.6x. The gap between trailing and forward, roughly 10% to 14% depending on whose forecast is used, is about the profit growth analysts expect over the next year. The trailing number rests on reported results; the forward number rests on forecasts, and consensus forecasts for FY27 earnings were cut by about 9% over the year to August 2026 (JM Financial).

**7. Gains from a rising P/E reversed after 2019, while gains from growing profits have held.**
In the first half of the decade, investors paid 21% more for each rupee of profit by 2019 than in 2016, and that extra has since been given back as the P/E came down. In the second half, the index went up because the companies in it now earn more than twice as much as they did in December 2019. The first kind of gain depends on how investors feel about the market; the second depends on what the companies actually earn.

## A note on dividends

The Nifty figures here track share prices only and leave out dividends. NSE's total-return index, which assumes every dividend is reinvested, grew 12.39% a year compounded over the ten years to 17 July 2026, against 11.04% a year for the price index, a gap of about 1.35 percentage points a year.

## What this means for a conversation with a client

For a client who asks whether the Nifty is expensive, today's numbers show it priced below its usual level. Investors are paying about 19 times the profit these companies actually reported, below the 22 times typical of the last five years, and near the bottom of the ten-year range on a like-for-like basis; only the late-2016 and early-2020 lows were cheaper. The ten-year gain came from profits more than doubling. A low P/E describes only today's price, and readings went lower still in 2016 and 2020, so prices can fall further in the months ahead even from here.

## How much to trust these numbers

- NSE's month-end P/E values come from Downstox, which republishes NSE Indices data. We checked 25 month-end values, plus the 30 March 2021 daily reading, against NSE's own monthly Index Dashboards and Nippon India Mutual Fund's notes. All but one agree within 0.05, which is Downstox's one-decimal rounding; the largest gap is 0.08, in July 2026, against a Nippon India product note. The 28 September 2026 value (19.26) is a daily reading from Nippon India's note citing NSE, since September's month-end figure is not out yet.
- The Nifty closes come from Yahoo Finance and were copied twice independently. All 2,483 days in Yahoo's daily table matched; the one missing day, 29 March 2019, was filled from Yahoo's monthly table, making 2,484 days in all.
- The like-for-like figures depend on how much bigger consolidated profit was than standalone profit before 2021, and estimates differ. Bloomberg data put the gap at 14% on average over the ten years to 2020 and at about 13% to 16% in early 2021 (as reported by The Hindu BusinessLine), and NSE's own switch-day numbers give 20.5%. PrimeInvestor put it at only about 5% in December 2019. We use 14% to 20.5%, the range best supported by NSE's own data and Bloomberg. At 5%, the decade's profit multiple would be about 3.1x, today's P/E would be 14% below September 2016's, and only the March 2020 low would have been cheaper than today. The workbook's Summary sheet shows all three cases.
- As a rough cross-check, Motilal Oswal's figures for Nifty earnings per share, from reports nine years apart, go from about ₹393 in FY16 (the year to March 2016) to ₹1,065 in FY26, 2.7 times, consistent with the range above. These are fiscal-year totals on Motilal Oswal's own method, so they will not match NSE's trailing figures exactly.
